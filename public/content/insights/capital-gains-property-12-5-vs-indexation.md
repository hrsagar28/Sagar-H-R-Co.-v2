Since 23 July 2024, long-term capital gains on the sale of land or a building are taxed at 12.5% without indexation, in place of 20% with indexation. Resident individuals and HUFs selling property they bought before that date pay the lower of the two. This note explains how the comparison works, when each side wins, and the other points to check on a property sale.

:::summary

### In short

- Long-term gains on land or a building transferred on or after 23 July 2024 are taxed at 12.5%, with no indexation.
- For a resident individual or HUF selling land or a building acquired before 23 July 2024, the tax cannot exceed what it would have been at 20% with indexation.
- Non-residents, companies, firms, LLPs and other entities pay 12.5% without indexation.
- The same rules continue under section 197 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 for sales from 1 April 2026.
  :::

## The rule

Land or a building held for more than 24 months is a long-term capital asset. When it is sold, the gain is the sale price less the cost of acquisition, the cost of any improvement, and the expenses of the sale. Indexation, the adjustment of cost for inflation, is no longer allowed. Tax is 12.5% of the gain, plus surcharge where applicable and 4% cess.

This is section 112 of the 1961 Act. From 1 April 2026 it is section 197 of the 2025 Act.

## The comparison for resident individuals and HUFs

For a resident individual or HUF selling land or a building acquired before 23 July 2024, any tax at 12.5% above the tax at 20% with indexation is ignored. In effect, the seller pays the lower of:

- 12.5% of the gain worked out without indexation, and
- 20% of the gain worked out with indexed cost.

This is the second proviso to section 112(1)(a) of the 1961 Act, and section 197(3) of the 2025 Act. It applies on its own; no option has to be exercised.

The test is residential status in the year of sale, not citizenship. An overseas citizen of India who is resident in that year qualifies; an Indian citizen who is non-resident does not.

If the indexed working shows a loss, the tax under the comparison is nil. The loss itself cannot be set off or carried forward, because the gain is still worked out without indexation.

For property acquired before 1 April 2001, the seller can take its fair market value on 1 April 2001 as the cost. That value cannot be more than the stamp duty value of the land or building on that date.

## Which side is lower

It depends on how long the property was held, because that decides how much indexation raises the cost. The 12.5% side is lower when the indexed gain is more than 62.5% of the gain without indexation.

As a price ratio: for a sale in FY 2026-27 (cost inflation index 384), the 12.5% side is lower only if the sale price is more than these multiples of the cost:

| Year of purchase                  | Sale price must exceed |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| 2001-02, or earlier at 2001 value | 8.6 times the cost     |
| 2005-06                           | 7.1 times              |
| 2010-11                           | 4.5 times              |
| 2015-16                           | 2.4 times              |
| 2020-21                           | 1.7 times              |

**Example.** A resident bought a plot in FY 2010-11 for ₹35 lakh and sells it in FY 2026-27 for ₹1.40 crore, with no other costs.

- At 12.5% without indexation: the gain is ₹1.05 crore, and the tax is ₹13.13 lakh.
- At 20% with indexation: the indexed cost is ₹35 lakh × 384 ÷ 167 = ₹80.48 lakh. The gain is ₹59.52 lakh, and the tax is ₹11.90 lakh.

The resident pays ₹11.90 lakh. A non-resident selling the same plot pays ₹13.13 lakh. Both figures are before surcharge and cess.

## Stamp duty value

If the stamp duty value of the property is more than 110% of the agreed price, the stamp duty value is taken as the sale price (section 50C of the 1961 Act, section 78 of the 2025 Act). Where part of the price was received by a banking channel on or before the date of the agreement, the stamp duty value on that date can be used instead.

## Saving tax by reinvesting

These exemptions continue under the new Act:

- **Section 54 (section 82 of the 2025 Act).** For a gain on a residential house invested in another residential house in India, bought within one year before or two years after the sale, or built within three years. The exemption can cover two houses, once in a lifetime, if the gain is not more than ₹2 crore.
- **Section 54F (section 86).** For a gain on any other long-term asset, including a plot, invested in a residential house within the same periods. The exemption is in proportion to the net sale price invested.
- **Section 54EC (section 85).** For a gain on land or a building invested within six months in specified bonds. The cap is ₹50 lakh, and the bonds are locked in for five years.

For sections 54 and 54F, the cost of the new house counted for the exemption is capped at ₹10 crore. An amount not invested by the due date of the return can be deposited in the Capital Gains Accounts Scheme and used within the time allowed.

Because the gain is now worked out without indexation, it can be larger, and so can the amount needed to cover it under sections 54 and 54EC. Section 54F is based on the net sale price, so this does not affect it.

## TDS on the sale

- **Resident seller.** If the price or the stamp duty value is ₹50 lakh or more, the buyer deducts 1% of the higher figure.
- **Non-resident seller.** The buyer deducts tax at 12.5% plus surcharge and 4% cess, which comes to 13%, 14.3% or 14.95% depending on the amount. In law, tax is deductible only on the part of the payment that is taxable, which is the gain. In practice, buyers deduct on the full price unless the seller obtains a lower deduction certificate before the payment is made. Under the 2025 Act the application is made in Form 128 under section 395.
- **No TAN for individual buyers.** From 1 October 2026, a resident individual or HUF buying from a non-resident does not need a TAN to deduct and pay this tax.
